# Hypothesis Framing Skill

This skill provides a structured approach to transforming problem insights and validated assumptions into clear, falsifiable hypotheses that can be tested through experimentation and evidence gathering.

## When to Use This Skill

- After problem discovery and validation
- Following assumption mapping and validation
- Before designing experiments to test venture ideas
- When preparing to communicate venture concepts to stakeholders
- To create a shared understanding of what we believe to be true

## Core Principles

1. **Hypotheses must be testable** - They must be capable of being supported or refuted by evidence
2. **Falsifiability is key** - A good hypothesis specifies what would count as evidence against it
3. **Clear and specific language** - Avoid vague terms and ambiguous statements
4. **Based on evidence, not intuition** - Ground hypotheses in discovered problems and validated assumptions
5. **One hypothesis, one test** - Each hypothesis should be testable with a single, coherent experiment

## Hypothesis Components

A well-framed hypothesis contains three essential elements:

### 1. Population/Entity
- Who or what the hypothesis is about
- Specific enough to be identifiable and measurable
- Examples: "Small business owners with websites," "Mobile app users aged 18-35," "Restaurant owners in urban areas"

### 2. Behavior/Outcome
- What specific action, change, or result we expect to observe
- Observable and measurable when possible
- Examples: "will pay $20/month for," "will reduce task time by 50%," "will increase conversion rate by 15%"

### 3. Condition/Context
- The specific circumstances under which we expect the behavior to occur
- Includes the intervention, solution, or condition being tested
- Examples: "when presented with our accessibility auditing tool," "after using our time-tracking application for 2 weeks," "when offered our premium pricing tier"

## Hypothesis Statement Format

[Population/Entity] [will/will not] [behavior/outcome] [condition/context].

## Examples

### Desirability Hypotheses
- "Small business owners will pay $30/month for an accessibility auditing tool when they face potential legal compliance risks."
- "Mobile app users will share our fitness challenge with friends when offered in-app rewards for both parties."
- "Restaurant owners will switch to our reservation system when it reduces no-shows by 25% or more."

### Viability Hypotheses
- "Our subscription pricing model will generate sufficient revenue to cover costs when we achieve 500 paying customers."
- "We will achieve profitability within 18 months when customer acquisition cost remains below $30 and lifetime value exceeds $180."
- "Our channel partnership strategy will be viable when partners earn at least $20 per customer referred."

### Feasibility Hypotheses
- "We can build a minimum viable product in 6 weeks with our current team using the selected technology stack."
- "Our proposed architecture will handle 10,000 concurrent users with acceptable response times."
- "We can integrate with the top 3 payment processors in the market using their standard APIs."

## Hypothesis Framing Process

### 1. Preparation
- Gather problem insights from customer interviews and research
- Review validated assumptions from assumption mapping
- Identify the key belief we want to test
- Prepare hypothesis statement templates

### 2. Insight Synthesis

#### Problem-Hypothesis Connection
- Link specific problem insights to potential solutions
- Identify which problem aspects our hypothesis addresses
- Ensure hypothesis targets a real, validated problem

#### Assumption-Hypothesis Connection
- Identify which validated assumptions support the hypothesis
- Ensure hypothesis doesn't contradict any validated assumptions
- Use assumptions as building blocks for the hypothesis

#### Gap Identification
- Note what we still need to validate to have confidence in the hypothesis
- Identify assumptions that remain unvalidated but are referenced

### 3. Hypothesis Crafting

#### Start with the Population
- Be specific about who/what we're making a claim about
- Use language from customer interviews when possible
- Avoid overly broad or vague population definitions

### Specify the Behavior
- Choose observable, measurable actions or outcomes
- Focus on behaviors that indicate value, adoption, or success
- Consider both positive and negative framings (will/will not)

### Define the Condition
- Clearly state what we're providing or changing
- Make the condition specific and replicable
- Consider what the control or comparison condition would be

### 4. Hypothesis Evaluation

#### Testability Check
- Can we design an experiment to test this hypothesis?
- What would count as evidence for or against it?
- Is the hypothesis specific enough to yield clear results?

#### Falsifiability Check
- Does the hypothesis specify what would disprove it?
- Are we open to either outcome (true or false)?
- Does it avoid tautological or unfalsifiable statements?

#### Clarity Check
- Is the hypothesis easy to understand and communicate?
- Does it avoid jargon, acronyms, or ambiguous terms?
- Could someone unfamiliar with the venture understand it?

#### Relevance Check
- Does the hypothesis address a key uncertainty or risk?
- Is it aligned with our validated learning to date?
- Would testing this hypothesis move us forward significantly?

### 5. Hypothesis Documentation

#### Hypothesis Record
- Record the exact hypothesis statement
- Note the date and team members involved
- Document the problem insights and assumptions it's based on
- Record any alternative formulations considered

#### Context Information
- Link to relevant problem statements and insight summaries
- Reference the assumptions that support or relate to the hypothesis
- Note any dependencies on other hypotheses

## Evidence Collection

- Record hypotheses in the exact words formulated
- Note the problem insights and assumptions that led to each hypothesis
- Capture the reasoning behind specific phrasing choices
- Label hypotheses as exploratory (early) or validation (later stage)
- Track changes to hypotheses over time

## Skill Overview

The hypothesis framing skill enables practitioners to transform problem insights and validated assumptions into clear, testable hypotheses that can be validated through experimentation and evidence. Unlike vague ideas or untested beliefs, this approach creates specific, falsifiable statements that define exactly what we believe to be true about a venture, enabling rigorous validation efforts.

## Key Differentiators

- Focuses on transforming insights and assumptions into specific statements
- Requires hypotheses to be testable and falsifiable through evidence
- Structures hypotheses around population, behavior, and condition components
- Grounds hypotheses in validated learning rather than intuition
- Enables clear communication of what we believe to be true

## Common Applications

- Converting problem insights into testable hypotheses
- Preparing for experiment design and validation planning
- Communicating venture concepts to team members and stakeholders
- Building investor-ready hypothesis statements
- Creating a shared understanding of venture beliefs

## Success Indicators

After applying this skill, you should be able to:
- Frame hypotheses that include specific population, behavior, and condition
- Ground hypotheses in specific problem insights and validated assumptions
- Ensure hypotheses are testable and falsifiable through evidence
- Consider and document alternative hypothesis formulations
- Communicate hypotheses clearly to unfamiliar audiences

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- Framing hypotheses that are vague or untestable
- Basing hypotheses on intuition rather than evidence
- Creating hypotheses that contradict validated assumptions
- Using jargon or unclear language that impedes communication
- Failing to specify what would count as evidence for or against the hypothesis
- Creating hypotheses that are too broad or complex to test meaningfully

## Next Steps

After completing hypothesis framing, consider:
- Designing experiments to test the framed hypotheses
- Creating validation plans based on hypothesis success criteria
- Developing assumption maps from hypotheses requiring validation
- Building minimum viable products to test feasibility hypotheses
- Sharing framed hypotheses with the venture team and advisors
