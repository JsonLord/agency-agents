# Evidence Audit Skill

This skill provides a structured approach to evaluating the quality, credibility, and relevance of evidence used in venture validation work. Unlike accepting evidence at face value, this skill emphasizes critical evaluation of evidence sources, collection methods, and analytical processes to ensure validation efforts are based on trustworthy information.

## When to Use This Skill

- After collecting evidence from interviews, experiments, or research
- Before making decisions based on evidence
- When evaluating the strength of support for hypotheses or assumptions
- To identify potential biases, limitations, or weaknesses in evidence
- To ensure validation efforts are based on credible information

## Core Principles

1. **Evidence quality matters more than quantity** - A few high-quality pieces beat many low-quality ones
2. **Source credibility is essential** - Who or what produced the evidence affects its trustworthiness
3. **Collection methods influence results** - How evidence was gathered affects what we can conclude
4. **Analytical processes affect interpretation** - How evidence was analyzed affects what we learn from it
5. **Context determines relevance** - Evidence must be relevant to the specific hypothesis or assumption
6. **Bias is inevitable but manageable** - We must identify and account for potential biases
7. **Negative evidence is valuable** - Evidence that contradicts expectations is often the most informative

## Evidence Dimensions Framework

When evaluating evidence, consider these dimensions:

### 1. Validity
- Does the evidence actually measure what it claims to measure?
- Is there a logical connection between the evidence and the conclusion?
- Are there alternative explanations for the evidence?

### 2. Reliability
- Would we get similar results if we repeated the evidence collection?
- Is the evidence consistent across different measurements or observers?
- Does the evidence show stability over time (when appropriate)?

### 3. Relevance
- Is the evidence relevant to the specific hypothesis or assumption being tested?
- Does it address the right population, behavior, and condition?
- Is it timely and applicable to the current context?

### 4. Credibility
- How trustworthy is the source of the evidence?
- What are the source's qualifications, expertise, and potential biases?
- Is the source transparent about methods and limitations?

### 5. Completeness
- Does the evidence tell the full story, or are important aspects missing?
- Are there significant gaps in the data or reasoning?
- Does evidence selectively support a particular conclusion?

### 6. Bias Awareness
- What potential biases could affect the evidence?
- How have biases been identified, measured, or accounted for?
- Are efforts made to minimize or counteract known biases?

## Evidence Types and Their Characteristics

### 1. Direct Behavioral Evidence
- **Examples**: Actual purchases, usage data, observed behaviors
- **Strengths**: High validity, difficult to fake, objective measurement
- **Limitations**: May lack context, can be expensive to collect
- **Best for**: Testing willingness to pay, actual adoption, usage patterns

### 2. Self-Reported Behavioral Evidence
- **Examples**: Survey responses about past behavior, diaries, logs
- **Strengths**: Can capture detailed experiences, relatively easy to collect
- **Limitations**: Subject to memory biases, social desirability, inaccuracies
- **Best for**: Understanding experiences, motivations, and contexts when direct observation isn't feasible

### 3. Opinion and Attitude Evidence
- **Examples**: Survey responses about preferences, attitudes, beliefs
- **Strengths**: Good for measuring subjective states, easy to collect at scale
- **Limitations**: Subject to biases, may not predict actual behavior, hypothetical
- **Best for**: Measuring preferences, attitudes, and subjective experiences

### 4. Expert Opinion Evidence
- **Examples**: Consultant reports, industry analysis, academic research
- **Strengths**: Can provide specialized knowledge, save time on research
- **Limitations**: Varies by expert expertise, may have conflicts of interest
- **Best for**: Gaining domain knowledge, understanding trends, technical assessments

### 5. Analogous or Comparative Evidence
- **Examples**: Case studies, benchmarking, industry reports
- **Strengths**: Can provide insights when direct evidence is lacking
- **Limitations**: May not be directly applicable, differences may be significant
- **Best for**: Understanding patterns, learning from others' experiences, setting benchmarks

### 6. Theoretical or Model-Based Evidence
- **Examples**: Financial projections, mathematical models, simulations
- **Strengths**: Can explore scenarios, test assumptions, plan resources
- **Limitations**: Based on assumptions, may not reflect reality, complex to validate
- **Best for**: Planning, forecasting, understanding relationships, sensitivity analysis

## Evidence Audit Process

### 1. Preparation
- Clearly state the hypothesis or assumption being evaluated
- Gather all evidence to be audited
- Identify the specific questions we want to answer about the evidence
- Prepare evidence audit templates

### 2. Source Evaluation

#### Who/What Produced the Evidence?
- Is it a primary source (direct observation/measurement) or secondary (analysis/summary)?
- What are the source's qualifications, expertise, and track record?
- Does the source have any potential conflicts of interest or biases?
- Is the source transparent about limitations and methodology?

#### How Was the Evidence Produced?
- What methods were used to collect or create the evidence?
- Are the methods appropriate to the question being asked?
- Were the methods applied correctly and consistently?
- Are there known limitations or weaknesses in the methods?

### 3. Content Evaluation

#### What Does the Evidence Actually Say?
- Extract the factual content, separating it from interpretation or opinion
- Identify specific claims, measurements, or observations made
- Note any qualifications, limitations, or conditions attached

#### How Strong is the Evidence for the Claim?
- Evaluate the logical connection between evidence and claim
- Consider alternative explanations for the evidence
- Assess the possibility of chance, bias, or error

### 4. Context Evaluation

#### Is the Evidence Relevant?
- Does it address the right population, behavior, and condition?
- Is it from the right time period or applicable to current conditions?
- Does it match the context of the hypothesis or assumption being tested?

#### What Evidence is Missing?
- What important aspects or perspectives are not covered?
- What alternative explanations or counter-evidence might exist?
- What would we ideally like to see to increase confidence?

### 5. Synthesis and Judgment

#### Overall Evidence Quality
- Weigh the strengths and weaknesses across all dimensions
- Consider the evidence in relation to the specific hypothesis or assumption
- Determine what level of confidence the evidence warrants

#### Evidence Gap Analysis
- Identify what we still need to know to have confidence
- Determine what additional evidence would be most valuable
- Consider the cost-benefit of gathering additional evidence

## Evidence Evaluation Framework

### Validity Assessment

#### Construct Validity
- Does the evidence measure the theoretical construct it claims to?
- Example: Does a survey about "satisfaction" actually measure satisfaction?

#### Internal Validity
- Are we confident that the observed effect is due to our intervention?
- Have we controlled for confounding variables and alternative explanations?

#### External Validity
- Can we generalize the results to other populations, settings, or times?
- How representative is our sample or context of the broader population?

### Reliability Assessment

#### Test-Retest Reliability
- Would we get similar results if we repeated the measurement?
- Appropriate for stable characteristics over short time periods

#### Inter-Rater Reliability
- Would different observers or coders get similar results?
- Appropriate for observational or subjective measurements

#### Internal Consistency
- Do different parts of the evidence measure the same thing consistently?
- Appropriate for scales, indices, or multi-item measurements

### Relevance Assessment

#### Population Relevance
- Does the evidence involve the right target audience or population?
- Are we generalizing from a group that may not be representative?

#### Behavioral Relevance
- Does the evidence measure the specific behavior we're interested in?
- Are we measuring proxies or proxies of proxies instead of the real behavior?

#### Temporal Relevance
- Is the evidence from a time period that is relevant to our current question?
- Are we using outdated data to make decisions about current conditions?

#### Contextual Relevance
- Does the evidence match the specific context of our hypothesis or assumption?
- Are we applying evidence from one context to make claims about another?

### Credibility Assessment

#### Source Expertise
- Does the source have the necessary qualifications or expertise?
- Is the source recognized as knowledgeable in the relevant area?

#### Source Trustworthiness
- Does the source have a track record of accuracy and honesty?
- Have there been instances of bias, error, or misrepresentation?

#### Transparency
- Is the source open about methods, limitations, and potential biases?
- Can we evaluate the quality of the evidence based on disclosed information?

#### Conflict of Interest Disclosure
- Has the source disclosed any potential conflicts of interest?
- Are there relationships that could affect the objectivity of the evidence?

## Evidence Collection Audit

### Sampling and Recruitment

#### Target Population
- Did the evidence collection target the right population?
- Were there significant exclusions or biases in who was included?

#### Sampling Method
- Was the sampling method appropriate to the goals and population?
- Was the sampling method applied correctly and consistently?

#### Recruitment Process
- How were participants found and enlisted?
- Were there biases in who agreed to participate?
- Were incentives appropriate and not coercive?

### Data Collection

#### Measurement Tools
- Were the tools appropriate to what was being measured?
- Were the tools used correctly and consistently?
- Were the tools calibrated, validated, or checked for accuracy?

#### Collection Consistency
- Was the data collection procedure followed consistently?
- Were there significant deviations or changes in how data was collected?

#### Recording Practices
- Was data recorded accurately, completely, and in a timely manner?
- Were there errors, omissions, or inconsistencies in data recording?

### Handling Missing Data

#### Missing Data Patterns
- Is missing data random or systematic (related to specific characteristics)?
- What proportion of data is missing and in what areas?

#### Missing Data Handling
- How was missing data addressed (deletion, imputation, model-based)?
- Were the methods appropriate and documented?
- Did the handling introduce bias or distort the results?

## Evidence Analysis Audit

### Analytical Methods

#### Appropriateness
- Were the statistical or analytical methods appropriate to the data and question?
- Were the methods applied correctly according to their assumptions?

#### Rigor
- Were the analyses conducted with appropriate care and attention to detail?
- Were potential errors, outliers, or anomalies checked and addressed?

#### Transparency
- Are the analytical steps and decisions clearly documented?
- Can someone else follow and reproduce the analysis?

### Interpretation

#### Logical Connection
- Is there a clear, logical path from evidence to interpretation?
- Are we making leaps in logic or skipping steps in reasoning?

#### Alternative Explanations
- Have we seriously considered alternative explanations for the evidence?
- Are we ignoring or dismissing plausible alternatives without good reason?

#### Overinterpretation
- Are we claiming more than the evidence actually supports?
- Are we reading too much into limited or preliminary evidence?

#### Underinterpretation
- Are we failing to see what the evidence clearly shows?
- Are we ignoring strong patterns or clear trends in the data?

## Evidence Synthesis and Judgment

### Weighing Evidence

#### Consistency Check
- Do different pieces of evidence tell the same story?
- Are there significant contradictions or tensions in the evidence?

#### Quality Weighting
- Should we give more weight to higher-quality evidence?
- How do we balance quality, quantity, and relevance?

#### Contextual Fit
- Does the evidence fit well with what we already know from other sources?
- Does it create cognitive dissonance or require excessive explanation?

### Confidence Assessment

#### Evidence Strength
- Based on quality, relevance, and credibility, how strong is the evidence?
- What level of confidence does it warrant in the hypothesis or assumption?

#### Evidence Agreement
- Do multiple sources of evidence point to the same conclusion?
- Is there convergence or divergence across evidence types and sources?

#### Remaining Uncertainty
- What important questions remain unanswered after considering the evidence?
- What would we need to know to have higher confidence?

## Skill Overview

The evidence audit skill enables practitioners to systematically evaluate the quality, credibility, and relevance of evidence used in venture validation work. Unlike accepting evidence at face value or relying on intuition, this approach provides a structured framework for assessing evidence across multiple dimensions, ensuring that validation efforts are based on trustworthy information rather than misleading or incomplete information.

## Key Differentiators

- Evaluates evidence across six key dimensions: validity, reliability, relevance, credibility, completeness, and bias awareness
- Provides specific criteria and questions for evaluating evidence sources, collection methods, content, and analysis
- Distinguishes between different types of evidence and their characteristic strengths/weaknesses
- Emphasizes the importance of evidence quality over mere quantity
- Provides frameworks for synthesizing evidence and assessing confidence levels

## Common Applications

- Auditing evidence from customer interviews and surveys
- Evaluating evidence from experiments and prototypes
- Assessing evidence from market research and competitor analysis
- Reviewing evidence from financial models and projections
- Evaluating evidence from technical feasibility assessments

## Success Indicators

After applying this skill, you should be able to:
- Evaluate evidence sources for credibility, expertise, and potential biases
- Assess evidence collection methods for appropriateness and consistency
- Evaluate evidence content for validity and logical connection to claims
- Audit evidence relevance for population, behavior, and condition
- Evaluate evidence analysis methods for appropriateness and rigor
- Synthesize evidence considering consistency, quality weighting, and contextual fit
- Judge evidence confidence levels and identify remaining uncertainty

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- Accepting evidence at face value without any evaluation
- Focusing only on supporting evidence while ignoring contradictory evidence
- Overlooking potential biases in evidence sources or collection methods
- Failing to evaluate how evidence collection methods affect what we can measure
- Not assessing whether evidence is actually relevant to the hypothesis being tested
- Using evidence that is outdated or from an irrelevant context
- Relying on expert opinion without evaluating the expert's qualifications or biases
- Ignoring the analytical processes that turn raw evidence into conclusions

## Next Steps

After completing evidence audit, consider:
- Making decisions based on audited evidence rather than raw evidence
- Updating hypotheses and assumptions based on evidence quality and credibility
- Designing additional evidence collection to address gaps and weaknesses
- Communicating evidence quality and limitations to stakeholders
- Using audited evidence to build investor-ready validation cases
